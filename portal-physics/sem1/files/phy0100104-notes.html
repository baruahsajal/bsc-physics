1. Vector Differentiation

In physics, we frequently deal with quantities that vary in space and time. A field is a physical quantity associated with every point in a region of space. We classify these into Scalar Fields (e.g., temperature, electric potential) and Vector Fields (e.g., electric field, gravitational field).

1.1 The Del Operator ($\nabla$)

The vector differential operator $\nabla$ (read as "del" or "nabla") is central to vector calculus. In Cartesian coordinates, it is defined as:

$$ \nabla = \hat{i} \frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \hat{j} \frac{\partial}{\partial y} + \hat{k} \frac{\partial}{\partial z} $$

The operator itself does not have a physical meaning unless it acts upon a scalar or vector function.

2. Gradient, Divergence, and Curl

2.1 Gradient of a Scalar Field

Definition: The gradient of a scalar field $\phi(x,y,z)$ is a vector field that represents the maximum rate of change of the scalar field. It points in the direction of the steepest ascent.

Mathematically, it is expressed as:

$$ \text{grad } \phi = \nabla \phi = \hat{i} \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} + \hat{j} \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} + \hat{k} \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial z} $$

Physical Significance: In conservative force fields, the force is the negative gradient of potential energy: $\vec{F} = -\nabla U$.

2.2 Divergence of a Vector Field

Definition: The divergence of a vector field $\vec{A}$ at a point represents the net outward flux of the field per unit volume around that point.

If $\vec{A} = A_x \hat{i} + A_y \hat{j} + A_z \hat{k}$, then the divergence is a scalar quantity:

$$ \text{div } \vec{A} = \nabla \cdot \vec{A} = \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial A_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial z} $$

If $\nabla \cdot \vec{A} = 0$, the vector field is called solenoidal (e.g., Magnetic fields: $\nabla \cdot \vec{B} = 0$).

2.3 Curl of a Vector Field

Definition: The curl of a vector field $\vec{A}$ represents the rotation or the angular velocity of the field at a point.

It is evaluated as the determinant:

$$ \nabla \times \vec{A} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ A_x & A_y & A_z \end{vmatrix} $$

If $\nabla \times \vec{A} = 0$, the vector field is called irrotational (e.g., Electrostatic fields). A conservative force is always irrotational.

3. Fundamental Theorems of Vector Integration

3.1 Gauss's Divergence Theorem

This theorem relates a volume integral of the divergence of a vector field to a surface integral of the vector field over the closed boundary of the volume.

$$ \iiint_V (\nabla \cdot \vec{A}) \, dV = \iint_S \vec{A} \cdot d\vec{S} $$

3.2 Stokes' Theorem

Stokes' theorem connects the surface integral of the curl of a vector field over an open surface $S$ to the line integral of the vector field along the closed curve $C$ bounding the surface.

$$ \iint_S (\nabla \times \vec{A}) \cdot d\vec{S} = \oint_C \vec{A} \cdot d\vec{l} $$
